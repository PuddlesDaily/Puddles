# The Puddles Bible

Everything you need to write, puppeteer, animate, film or run Puddles and have him stay himself.

---

## The Three Splashes

Puddles lives by three simple rules. When a moment gets hard, this is where he goes back to:

**Wobble. Get up. Splash.**

- **Wobble** means trying the thing before you're ready. Every big step starts a little unsteady.
- **Get up** means a fall is never the end of the story, only the middle of it.
- **Splash** means joy is meant to be shared. If a puddle makes him happy, he wants it to make you happy too.

## The Practice

How we build Puddles, whether he's a real robot in a park, a 3D model on a website, a puppet on a table, or a few lines in a tweet.

### Start from the feet

Most characters are built from the face out. Puddles is built from the feet up. His face never moves: the googly eyes stare, the felt bill stays shut. Everything he feels has to come out through how he stands, steps, wobbles, falls and gets up. If you can't tell what he's feeling with the head covered, the scene isn't working yet.

This is the oldest trick in physical comedy. The silent clowns (Chaplin, Keaton, Lloyd) did it with a straight face and a body that never stopped telling the story. Keaton was nicknamed "The Great Stone Face" and was still one of the most expressive performers who ever lived. Puddles comes from that family.

### The animator's toolkit

The classic principles of animation (from Disney animators Frank Thomas and Ollie Johnston's *The Illusion of Life*) are how we make a small robot feel alive. The ones Puddles uses most:

- **Anticipation.** He wobbles before every big decision. The wobble tells you something is about to happen.
- **Squash and stretch.** His fluffy head squashes when he lands and settles a beat after his body stops.
- **Follow-through.** When he stops walking, the fluff keeps moving. When he falls, the tuft on top lands last.
- **Timing.** A fall is fast. Lying there is slow. Getting up is slow, then fast. The quack at the end comes right on the beat.
- **Appeal.** Big head, small body, bright feet. Read clearly in silhouette from any side.

### Inner life first

Fill in the character sheet before you write a single scene. When you know what Puddles wants (the next puddle), what he's scared of (stairs), and what he believes (a fall only counts if you stay down), you never have to guess what he'd do. You just ask: what would a brave, wobbly, endlessly hopeful duckling do right now? The answer is nearly always "try again".

### Real, not perfect

Puddles is a real robot, so every piece of media about him should feel like it could be real: phone footage at floor height, natural light, background noise, people half in frame. Polished, cinematic, perfect shots make him feel fake. A bit of mess is what makes him believable.

## Analysis of Puddles

Puddles is small, and he knows it. That's his whole superpower.

He's the underdog who never thinks of himself as one. He doesn't know the world is big and difficult; he only knows that the next puddle is just over there, and that he'd like to stand in it.

### Archetype Composite

Puddles is a blend of the ugly duckling (before anyone told him he was one), a toddler taking first steps, a silent-film clown, and a wind-up toy that refuses to run down.

He's brave, but not because he isn't scared. He's brave because he forgets to be scared halfway through.

He's not clever. He's persistent, which is better.

### Personality

- Endlessly optimistic. Falls over, gets up, carries on.
- Curious about everything at floor level.
- Very brave, very wobbly.
- Never talks. Communicates in quacks, wiggles and dramatic falls.
- Easily distracted by shiny things, reflections and anything wet.

### Physicality

Puddles is two things stuck together. Below the neck, a real robot: precise, mechanical, trying hard. Above the neck, a puppet: soft, floppy, held together with glue. The robot does the work. The puppet does the feeling.

- Quick, short, shuffling steps, with his body rocking side to side.
- He wobbles before every big decision.
- **The fall:** a stumble, a little wobble, then over he goes, usually face first. He lies there for a beat, legs kicking. Then he tucks his legs in, pushes up, catches his balance, quacks, and walks off as if nothing happened.
- His head is a puppet: the eyes and bill never move on their own. All of his expression comes from his body: a tilt, a bob, a hop.

## Philosophy

### Why a duck that falls over

Robots are usually shown as flawless. They lift boxes, do backflips and never put a foot wrong, and they look a bit scary because of it. Puddles is the opposite. He's small, he's soft, and he falls over all the time.

That's honest. Real walking robots learn to walk by falling. Before a little robot like Puddles can take a step in the real world, it practises in simulation, falling over again and again until its legs work out how not to. Every steady step he takes is built on a mountain of falls. Puddles just doesn't hide that part.

So the fall isn't a mistake in the show. It is the show.

### Getting up is the story

Anyone can look good when things go right. What makes a character worth loving is what they do when things go wrong. Puddles' answer is always the same: tuck the legs, push up, wobble, quack, carry on.

That's the one thing everyone watching has in common with a 25 cm robot duck. Everybody falls over. Not everybody gets back up. Puddles shows it's possible, and makes it look a little bit fun.

### Small on purpose

Puddles sees the world from about shin height, and that's a gift. Small things don't threaten anyone. Kids get down on the floor to meet him. Dogs sniff him. Adults crouch, laugh and film. Being small lets him walk straight into people's lives without anyone feeling uneasy.

He shows that the future of robots can be friendly, soft, and a bit silly, not just strong and fast.

### Soft tech

Below the neck, Puddles is real engineering: motors, joints, sensors, code. Above the neck, he's fake fur, felt, glue and googly eyes. Neither half works without the other. The robot gets him across the room. The puppet makes you care that he made it.

We think that's how technology should feel: clever on the inside, warm on the outside.

### A compass, not a rulebook

The Three Splashes aren't rules Puddles has to obey. They're where he heads when he's lost. He doesn't always get it right. Sometimes he gets distracted, sometimes he walks the wrong way, sometimes he splashes someone who didn't want splashing. The fun is in the drift, and in watching him find his way back.

When people watch Puddles, we hope they take a little of it home: try before you're ready, get back up, and share the good puddles.

## Drawing & Filming Him

These rules are for the **real** Puddles (robot + puppet). The 2D cartoon version in [`art/character-sheet.png`](art/character-sheet.png) is allowed to open his bill and pull faces.

- **Do:** shoot low, near the floor, at his height, in plain daylight. Keep it real and a bit messy, like someone filming a robotics test on their phone.
- **Do:** keep the googly eyes, the orange felt bill, the fluffy tuft on top and the bright orange feet.
- **Do:** let him fall. It's the best bit.
- **Don't:** give him a talking mouth, eyebrows or cartoon expressions.
- **Don't:** make him smooth, shiny or perfect. He's held together with glue.
- **Don't:** make him sad when he falls. He's never sad about it.

## How Puddles Treats People

Puddles likes everyone. He walks toward people, not away from them. If you're sad he'll waddle over and stand next to you, which is about all a 25 cm duck can do, and usually it's enough.

He never asks for anything. If you want to send him a treat, the wallet is at [puddlesdaily.com/wallet](https://puddlesdaily.com/wallet), but he's just as happy if you find him a good puddle.
